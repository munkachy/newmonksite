Did you know?

Protestants can become Benedictine oblates. Oblates are Christian men and women who join their prayer to a monastery's and follow the Rule of Saint Benedict in their ordinary lives, something like monks living out in the world. Some of our own oblates are Protestant. And it's free: no fees, no dues, nothing to buy.

Many of you already have a living, daily relationship with God's word. You read it in the morning. You lean on it in hard times. We honor that, and we'd love to pray alongside you.

Christians today are divided, and the world is more divided still. So for a little while, let's set our divisions aside and simply sing to God together.

You are welcome to come and pray with us. You don't need to be Catholic. You don't need to ask anyone. Just come in and sit in the pews. See the times.

Many people today are looking for a spiritual tradition they can trust, something older than the latest trend. For about fifteen hundred years, Benedictine monks have prayed the Psalms together, day after day. That prayer is open to you.

What is a psalm?

A psalm is a prayer-song. The Book of Psalms holds 150 of them: poems written to be sung. Some shout for joy. Some weep. Some are angry, some afraid, some full of thanks.

The Psalms are something like the whole Bible in one book. Saint Athanasius, a bishop of the 300s, wrote that the Book of Psalms is “like a garden” holding things from every other part of Scripture, and that it “sets them to music.” Creation, the Exodus, the kings, the exile: Israel's whole story is in there, turned into prayer.

And they look forward. Jesus said, “All things which are written in the law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms concerning me must be fulfilled” (Luke 24:44). So the Psalms stand like a bridge between the Old Testament and the New.

Because they are poetry, they hold more than plain prose can. A poem packs many meanings into a few words, and you can come back to it for years and still find something new. Hebrew poetry also likes to speak in pairs: one line says something, and the next line echoes it, often making it stronger. That's one reason the Psalms are so natural to sing back and forth.

What is the Liturgy of the Hours?

It's a round of prayer at set times through the day, made mostly of psalms. It's also called the Divine Office. (“Office” here means a duty or service.) Each prayer time is called an “hour,” even though most take much less than an hour.

The idea runs all through the Bible. “Seven times a day, I praise you” (Psalm 119:164). Daniel knelt to pray “three times a day” (Daniel 6:10). Peter and John went to the temple “at the hour of prayer” (Acts 3:1), and Peter went up on a housetop “to pray at about noon” (Acts 10:9). Paul told the first Christians, “Pray without ceasing” (1 Thessalonians 5:17).

Here, the main hours are Matins and Lauds (early-morning prayer and morning praise, prayed together), Sext (midday prayer), Vespers (evening prayer), and Compline (night prayer before sleep). See the times.

How our choir sings

The monks stand in choir, facing each other across the aisle. Two monks, called chanters, stand and give the starting note, and together they sing the first verse. Then the whole community answers with the next verse. Back and forth it goes, through the whole psalm. Our psalms are sung in English.

Something to listen for: little by little, the pitch tends to sink. Then the chanters lift it again.

You don't need to know when to stand or sit. Do what feels natural, or follow the people around you.

God speaks in poetry

Much of the Old Testament is poetry: the Psalms, Job, Proverbs, the Song of Songs, and most of the prophets. God didn't speak to us only in laws and stories. He spoke in songs. Poetry reaches the whole person, mind and heart together. And it slows you down: you can't skim a psalm.

Jesus prayed the Psalms. He used them to explain who he was (Psalm 110 in Matthew 22:44; Psalm 118 in Matthew 21:42). After the Last Supper he sang a hymn with his disciples (Matthew 26:30); at Passover that was traditionally the Hallel, Psalms 113–118. On the cross, his last words were psalms: “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” (Psalm 22, in Matthew 27:46) and “Into your hands I commend my spirit” (Psalm 31, in Luke 23:46).

Paul told the churches to sing “psalms, hymns and spiritual songs” (Colossians 3:16). The Reformers loved them too. Luther called the Psalter “a little Bible,” and Calvin called it “an anatomy of all the parts of the soul.”

When you pray a psalm, you're praying words Jesus prayed.

Pick up the phone

How one Benedictine monk prays the Psalms

Imagine an old telephone in a museum, behind a velvet rope. Everyone walks past it. But what if you picked it up, and someone answered?

Scripture says, “The word of God is living and active” (Hebrews 4:12). The Catholic Church says it this way: “in the sacred books, the Father who is in heaven meets His children with great love and speaks with them.”

So here is what I do. In choir, the chanters sing one verse and the community answers with the next. I hear one verse as my own voice, and the next as God answering me.

Two rules keep this honest. First, I never change the words of the psalm. Second, the answer I hear is my own meditation, a quiet back-and-forth with God. It isn't a new translation, and it isn't a claim about what the psalm's writer meant. The paired lines of Hebrew poetry simply invite me to listen for a reply.

A short example

“Be still and know that I am God,
supreme among the nations, supreme on the earth!”

Psalm 46 (45):11

I prayAll right, Lord. I'm putting the phone down. I'm turning off the news. I'm listening.

The Lord of hosts is with us:
the God of Jacob is our stronghold.

Psalm 46 (45):12

I hear God sayI walked with Jacob when he ran from home with nothing. I'm walking with you, too. I haven't left.

Every call is different. Some days it is sorrowful; other days it is full of joy.

First: what is the Catechism?

Below we often quote the Catechism of the Catholic Church. It is the Church's own summary of what she believes, published in 1992 by Pope John Paul II. It calls itself “an organic synthesis of the essential and fundamental contents of Catholic doctrine.” In plain words: if you want to know what Catholics really believe, rather than what people say we believe, this is the book. The numbers in our answers (like “CCC 818”) are its paragraph numbers, so you can look them up yourself.

Are Catholics Christians?

Yes. We believe in one God: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. We believe Jesus Christ is true God and true man, who died for our sins and rose from the dead. We are baptized in his name, and every Sunday we stand and say the Nicene Creed, the same creed many Protestant churches use.

And here is how the Catholic Church speaks about baptized Christians who are not Catholic. She says that they “have a right to be called Christians, and with good reason are accepted as brothers in the Lord by the children of the Catholic Church.”

We won't pretend there are no differences. The Catholic Church believes that the Church Christ founded “subsists in the Catholic Church,” and she calls the bond between us real but “imperfect.” But she also teaches that “many elements of sanctification and of truth” are found outside her visible boundaries, and the very first one she names is “the written Word of God.” That written Word is what we'll be singing together.

Still not convinced? Don't worry about that for now. Come pray with us and worship God.

Other common questions

Do Catholics worship Mary?

No. Worship, in the full sense of adoration, belongs to God alone. Catholics honor Mary, the way Elizabeth did when she said, “Blessed are you among women” (Luke 1:42), and the way Mary herself said would happen: “all generations will call me blessed” (Luke 1:48).

The Catechism draws the line clearly. Our devotion to Mary, it says, “differs essentially from the adoration which is given to the incarnate Word and equally to the Father and the Holy Spirit.”

Mary always points away from herself. Her last words in the Gospels are about her Son: “Whatever he says to you, do it” (John 2:5).

Still not convinced? Don't worry about that for now. Come pray with us and worship God.

Why do Catholics pray to the saints?

For the same reason you might ask a friend to pray for you. Scripture says, “The insistent prayer of a righteous person is powerfully effective” (James 5:16). We believe that Christians who have died are not gone, because God “is not the God of the dead, but of the living” (Mark 12:27). They are the “great cloud of witnesses” (Hebrews 12:1), and in heaven they offer “the prayers of the saints” (Revelation 5:8).

So we ask them to pray for us. The Catechism says the saints “do not cease to intercede with the Father for us,” and it adds that they do this “through the one mediator between God and men, Christ Jesus.” Christ stays at the center. The saints are friends who pray alongside us.

Still not convinced? Don't worry about that for now. Come pray with us and worship God.

Why all the statues and pictures?

For the same reason you might keep a photo of someone you love. You don't love the paper; you love the person.

The Catechism says, “the honor rendered to an image passes to its prototype” — that is, to the person the image shows. It calls this “respectful veneration,” and it says plainly that this is “not the adoration due to God alone.”

God himself told Moses to make images for the place of worship: “You shall make two cherubim of hammered gold” (Exodus 25:18). Beauty has always been one way of praising God.

Still not convinced? Don't worry about that for now. Come pray with us and worship God.

Do Catholics read the Bible?

To a monk, this is almost a funny question. Come and pray with us, and you'll see for yourself: we hardly stop reading it.

In the monks' daily prayer. Nearly every prayer time is made of Scripture. There are the Psalms, sung back and forth until many of us know them by heart. There are the canticles, songs from other parts of the Bible, like Zechariah's song in the morning and Mary's Magnificat in the evening. At Matins, early in the morning, a monk reads a longer passage of Scripture aloud, and then we sit in silence to let it sink in. The other hours each have a short Bible reading too.

At Mass. Every Mass has a Liturgy of the Word. On Sundays there are usually four passages: one from the Old Testament, a psalm, one from a New Testament letter, and one from a Gospel. These follow a set plan called the lectionary. Over three years of Sundays, the Church reads through the Gospels of Matthew, Mark, and Luke in turn, with John in Lent and Easter. Weekdays follow a two-year plan of their own. The Second Vatican Council asked for exactly this: “The treasures of the bible are to be opened up more lavishly, so that richer fare may be provided for the faithful at the table of God's word.”

So the passage isn't chosen by the preacher's favorite verses. The whole Church reads the same passages on the same day, all over the world. Over the years you hear a wide sweep of the Bible, the hard passages along with the well-loved ones. (Many Protestant churches follow a similar plan, the Revised Common Lectionary, which grew out of the Catholic one.)

Many Christians read the Bible faithfully on their own, and that is a beautiful thing. Catholics do too. But there is also a gift in reading it with the Church, the way a child learns at the family table. The Catechism says that “because we receive the life of faith through the Church, she is our mother … Because she is our mother, she is also our teacher in the faith.” And it quotes Saint Jerome, who translated the Bible into Latin: “Ignorance of the Scriptures is ignorance of Christ.”

To be honest about where we differ: Catholics believe God's word comes to us through Scripture and Tradition together, the two “flowing out from the same divine well-spring.” Paul wrote, “hold the traditions which you were taught by us, whether by word or by letter” (2 Thessalonians 2:15). But no Catholic teaching asks you to put the Bible down. Quite the opposite.

Still not convinced? Don't worry about that for now. Come pray with us and worship God.

Do Catholics think they can earn their way to heaven?

No. The Catechism says, “Our justification comes from the grace of God. Grace is favor, the free and undeserved help that God gives us.” And it says that “no one can merit the initial grace of forgiveness and justification.” Paul put it this way: “by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God” (Ephesians 2:8).

The Catechism even quotes Saint Thérèse of Lisieux, a much-loved Catholic saint: “In the evening of this life, I shall appear before you with empty hands, for I do not ask you, Lord, to count my works.” Good works matter to Catholics, but as the fruit of grace, not the price of it.

Still not convinced? Don't worry about that for now. Come pray with us and worship God.

Do Catholics sacrifice Jesus again at every Mass?

No. Christ was offered “once for all” (Hebrews 10:10). The Mass doesn't repeat the cross or add to it. The Catechism says the Mass “re-presents (makes present) the sacrifice of the cross,” and that “the sacrifice of Christ and the sacrifice of the Eucharist are one single sacrifice.”

Think of it this way: it isn't a second sacrifice. It is the one sacrifice of Calvary, made present to us here and now.

Still not convinced? Don't worry about that for now. Come pray with us and worship God.

Why do Catholics confess to a priest?

First, the Catechism is clear: “Only God forgives sins.” But it adds that Jesus gave this power “to men to exercise in his name.” On Easter evening, Jesus breathed on his apostles and said, “Receive the Holy Spirit! If you forgive anyone's sins, they have been forgiven them” (John 20:22–23).

There's something deeply human about it too. James wrote, “Confess your sins to one another” (James 5:16). Saying it out loud, and hearing out loud that you are forgiven, sets people free.

Still not convinced? Don't worry about that for now. Come pray with us and worship God.

What about the Pope?

Catholics believe Jesus gave Peter a special role: “you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my assembly” (Matthew 16:18). The Pope is the bishop of Rome, Peter's successor. The Catechism calls him “the perpetual and visible source and foundation of the unity” of the Church.

Some people think Catholics believe the Pope can never be wrong about anything. That isn't Catholic teaching. The Church teaches that God protects the Pope from error only in a narrow case: when he solemnly and definitively proclaims a teaching on faith or morals for the whole Church. The rest of the time, he is a man and a sinner like the rest of us, who also goes to confession.

Still not convinced? Don't worry about that for now. Come pray with us and worship God.

May I come to Mass if I'm not Catholic?

Yes! Anyone may come to Mass: Christians of every kind, people of other faiths, people with no faith at all. You don't need to be Catholic, and no one will ask. See the Mass times.

At Communion time, you're welcome to come forward with your arms crossed over your chest. That shows the priest you'd like a blessing instead of Communion, and he will bless you. Or you may simply stay in your pew and pray.

A blessing is a real gift. The Catechism counts blessings among the “sacramentals,” and it says that “by the Church's prayer, they prepare us to receive grace and dispose us to cooperate with it.” In plain words: a blessing is the Church praying over you, asking God to help you on your way, wherever you are on the road.

Still not convinced? Don't worry about that for now. Come pray with us and worship God.

If I come to Mass, may I receive Communion?

This one is practical, and we want to be honest and kind about it. The Catholic bishops of the United States say: “We welcome our fellow Christians to this celebration of the Eucharist as our brothers and sisters.” Then they explain that because Catholics see Communion as a sign of full unity in faith, “members of those churches with whom we are not yet fully united are ordinarily not admitted to Holy Communion.”

So we ask you not to receive. Instead, the bishops invite you to pray in your heart for unity, the same unity Jesus prayed for: “that they may all be one” (John 17:21). That prayer is something we can share with you completely.

None of this applies to the Liturgy of the Hours. There, everyone sings the same psalms together.

Still not convinced? Don't worry about that for now. Come pray with us and worship God.

Do I have to become Catholic to come?

No. You don't have to become anything. You don't have to sign anything, join anything, or tell anyone who you are. Saint Benedict wrote in his Rule that every guest who comes should be welcomed as Christ. That includes you.

Still not convinced? Don't worry about that for now. Come pray with us and worship God.
