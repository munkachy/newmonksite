# Relics

Photographs of the abbey's relics, taken on 1 November 2026 and brought in from the "Relics" folder in Google Drive:
122 photos, showing about 60 reliquaries and well over 100 relics. (The iPhone's Live Photo clips, the .MOV files,
were left out; each one matches a photo here. One photo, IMG_4960, was in Drive twice; it is kept once.)

- `originals/`: the photos exactly as taken. Only the GPS location was removed; the picture itself is untouched.
- `labeled/`: an upright copy of each photo with the saint's name, the words on the label, and how sure the reading
  is, written across the bottom. The same name is saved in the file's details (Title, Description, and Tags, which
  show in Windows "Properties" and in most photo apps).

Every reading comes from the photos alone. "Sure" means the label can be read; "likely" means part of it is hidden,
faded or abbreviated, but the reading is the natural one; "unsure" means it is a best guess. Please check the
uncertain ones against the relics themselves and against any authentication papers (*authentica*) the abbey keeps.
Those papers, if they survive, would also settle which Agapitus, Secundus, Innocent or Justina is meant.

## What the labels tell us about dates

A label that calls someone "B." (*Beatus*, Blessed) was written after that person was beatified and before they were
canonized. The labels here were written over some two hundred years:

- the Franciscan reliquary calls Angelo of Acri, Leonard of Port Maurice, Pacificus and Thomas of Cori "Blessed":
  between 1825 and 1839;
- the Uganda Martyrs "BB.": 1920–1964 · Conrad of Parzham: 1930–1934 · John Bosco: 1929–1934 ·
  Pius X: 1951–1954 · Elizabeth Ann Seton "Bd.": 1963–1975 · Pier Giorgio Frassati: 1990–2025;
- on the same linen board, St. Charbel and St. John Neumann are already "S.", so those labels are from 1977 or later.

So the linen board was filled over decades, not made all at once.

## The saints

### Our Lord and Our Lady

**The tomb of Our Lord.** "Ex Sepul. D.N." means "from the sepulchre of Our Lord", that is, the tomb in Jerusalem where Jesus was buried and from which he rose. There is no relic of his body, so a relic like this is a tiny chip of stone from the tomb, or a piece of cloth or stone touched to it, carried home by pilgrims. The site was identified in the time of the Emperor Constantine, who built the first Church of the Holy Sepulchre over it in the 4th century; many historians think it may well be the right place, though it cannot be proved. The Church does not claim more for such a relic than that it comes from, or has touched, that holy place.

**The Holy Nail.** Feast: none on today's calendar; the Passion is kept on Good Friday. The label "Ex S. Clavo D.N.I.C." means "from the Holy Nail of Our Lord Jesus Christ". A tradition going back to the late 4th century says St. Helena found the nails of the Crucifixion with the Cross, and several churches, among them Santa Croce in Gerusalemme in Rome, keep a nail venerated as one of them. A relic like this one is almost certainly not a piece of such a nail, but a cloth or a small copy touched to it, or at most a filing from it. The Church does not claim that any of these nails is genuine; it allows them to be honoured for what they point to, the death of Christ, and the honour goes to him, not to the iron.

**The Holy House of Loreto.** Feast: Our Lady of Loreto, 10 December (added to the General Roman Calendar in 2019). Since the 15th century the shrine at Loreto, on Italy's Adriatic coast, has held a small stone room said to be the house at Nazareth where Mary lived and the angel came to her. The pious tradition is that angels carried it from Nazareth in 1291, first to Trsat in Dalmatia and then to Loreto in 1294. Some historians think instead that the stones were brought by ship from the Holy Land in the time of the Crusades, possibly by a family named Angeli. "Domus Laur." means "the house of Loreto"; relics of this kind are usually a crumb of its stone or mortar, or a piece of cloth touched to its walls.

**The tomb of the Blessed Virgin Mary ("Ex Sepulchro B. V. M.").** Two thecas in the velvet case hold one: one beside St. John the Baptist (IMG_4892, 4893), one framed by St. Joseph, St. Anne, St. Peter and St. Paul (IMG_4901, 4902). This is not a relic of Mary's body, and that is not an accident. That Mary was taken up body and soul into heavenly glory is **dogma** (Pius XII, *Munificentissimus Deus*, 1950). The Church teaches that no bodily relics of her exist. Whether she died before her Assumption was deliberately left undefined. That she did, the "Dormition," is the older and more common view, but it remains **theological opinion**. What pilgrims could bring home was a fragment from the tomb venerated in Jerusalem, in the Kidron Valley beside Gethsemane, or a cloth touched to it. "Ex sepulchro" means "from the tomb." It is a relic of the place, a witness that her body was laid there and is no longer there.

**Our Lady's garment.** Feast: the Assumption, 15 August. "Ex Veste B. M. V." means "from the garment of the Blessed Virgin Mary". The Church teaches as dogma, defined by Pope Pius XII in 1950, that Mary was taken up body and soul into heaven at the end of her earthly life, so there are no relics of her body; relics of Mary are of things she is said to have worn or touched, or of her tomb. The best known are the veil kept at Chartres Cathedral since the 9th century and the girdle (belt) at Prato in Italy. The Church makes no claim that a given thread is truly from her clothing; what it honours is the woman the relic recalls.

### The saints, A to Z

**St. Agapitus, Martyr.** He shares a wooden box with St. Secundus and, most likely, St. Justina (IMG_4887–4889). The label gives only the name and "M." for martyr, and several early martyrs bore this name. The two best known are St. Agapitus of Palestrina (Praeneste), a boy martyred near Rome in the third century (feast 18 August), and St. Agapitus the deacon, killed with Pope St. Sixtus II in 258 (6 August). Small paper labels written by hand, set on silk in a wooden case, are typical of relics given out from Rome in the 1700s and 1800s. Many of those came from the Roman catacombs. So this may be either of these men or another martyr of the same name. Only the relic's papers could settle it.

**St. Agatha (died c. 251), virgin and martyr.** Feast: 5 February. She was martyred at Catania in Sicily, probably in the persecution of the Emperor Decius, and like St. Lucy she is named in the Roman Canon. The account of her death, written later, says that after she refused a Roman official her breasts were cut off, and that St. Peter came to her in prison and healed her before she died. Tradition says that a year later the people of Catania carried her veil out against a stream of lava from Mount Etna and the lava stopped; she is still the city's patron, and her relics are kept in its cathedral.

**St. Agnes (died c. 304?), virgin and martyr.** Feast: 21 January. Agnes was a Roman girl of about twelve or thirteen, says St. Ambrose, who was put to death for her faith and buried on the Via Nomentana, where a basilica was built over her tomb in the 4th century. The details of her trial and death in the later accounts are legend. On her feast two lambs are blessed in Rome, and their wool is woven into the pallium, the band worn by archbishops.

**St. Aloysius Gonzaga (1568–1591), religious.** Feast: 21 June. Aloysius was the eldest son of the Marquis of Castiglione, raised for a soldier's and courtier's life, and he had to fight his father for years before he was allowed to give his inheritance to his younger brother and join the Jesuits in 1585. In 1591, during an epidemic in Rome, he carried the sick to hospital and nursed them, caught the disease, and died at 23; St. Robert Bellarmine was his spiritual director. He was canonized in 1726 and is the patron of young people.

**St. Alphonsus Liguori (1696–1787), bishop and Doctor of the Church.** Feast: 1 August. A brilliant young lawyer in Naples, he is said to have left the law after losing a case through an oversight, and became a priest who preached to the poorest country people around Naples. In 1732 he founded the Redemptorists to do that work, and he later served unwillingly as bishop of Sant'Agata dei Goti. His books on moral theology taught confessors to be gentle and sensible rather than harsh, and his *Glories of Mary* and short prayer books were read for generations. The label "S. Alph. d. L." (of St. Alphonsus de Liguori) calls him "S." (Saint), so it was written after his canonization in 1839.

**St. Andrew, apostle (first century).** Feast: 30 November. Andrew was a fisherman from Bethsaida in Galilee, a disciple of John the Baptist, and, in John's Gospel, one of the first two to follow Jesus; he went at once to bring his brother Simon Peter. He is also the one who found the boy with five loaves and two fish (John 6:8–9). Tradition says he preached in Greece and was crucified at Patras; the X-shaped cross is a later, medieval tradition. Most of his relics have been at Amalfi in Italy since 1208, and Pope Paul VI returned his head to Patras in 1964.

**Bl. Angelo of Acri (1669–1739), now St. Angelo, priest.** Feast: 30 October. Angelo twice entered the Capuchin friars in Calabria and twice left before he stayed, at the third try. He became a well-known preacher of missions in the towns and villages of southern Italy, speaking simply so that ordinary people could follow him. He was beatified in 1825 and canonized in 2017.

**St. Anne (1st century B.C.), mother of the Virgin Mary.** Feast: 26 July (with St. Joachim). The Bible does not name Mary's parents; their names and story come from the *Protoevangelium of James*, a writing of the 2nd century that the Church never took into Scripture but that shaped Christian art and prayer. In it Anne grieves for years that she has no child, until an angel tells her she will bear a daughter, whom she gives to God. Churches in several places, among them Apt in France and Düren in Germany, have claimed relics of her body, but these rest on tradition alone. The label "s. Annae Matr." means "of St. Anne, Mother" (of the Blessed Virgin).

**St. Anselm (1033–1109), Benedictine monk, abbot and Doctor of the Church.** Feast: 21 April. Born at Aosta in northern Italy, he became a monk of Bec in Normandy under Lanfranc, then its prior and, from 1078, its abbot. In 1093 he was made Archbishop of Canterbury against his will and was twice sent into exile for standing up to the English kings William II and Henry I over the freedom of the Church. His motto was "faith seeking understanding", and in "Why God Became Man" he asked why our salvation needed the Son of God to become man and die. He was named a Doctor of the Church in 1720.

**St. Anthony of Padua (1195–1231), priest and Doctor of the Church.** Feast: 13 June. Born Fernando in Lisbon, he was an Augustinian canon until, moved by the bodies of five Franciscan friars martyred in Morocco, he became a Franciscan and set out to die for Christ in Africa himself; illness drove his ship back, and it landed in Italy. There his gift for preaching was discovered almost by accident, and he spent his last years preaching to great crowds in northern Italy and France. He died near Padua at about 36 and was canonized less than a year later. The label "Cin. S. Ant. Pat. C." means "ashes of St. Anthony of Padua, Confessor"; the collection also has a medal with no name on it, so nothing tells us whether it holds a relic of him.

**St. Augustine of Hippo (354–430), bishop and Doctor of the Church.** Feast: 28 August. Born in North Africa, the son of St. Monica, he spent his youth chasing success as a teacher of rhetoric, followed the Manichees for years, and lived with a woman who bore him a son. In Milan, through the preaching of St. Ambrose and after hearing a child's voice say "Take and read", he turned to Christ and was baptized at Easter 387. As bishop of Hippo for more than thirty years he wrote the *Confessions*, *The City of God* and a great deal more that has shaped Western Christianity ever since. He died while the Vandals were besieging his city.

**Bl. Beatrice I d'Este (c. 1192–1226) and Bl. Beatrice II d'Este (died 1262), Benedictine nuns.** Feasts: 10 May (Beatrice I) and 18 January (Beatrice II). Both were daughters of the house of Este, and both gave up the life of the court to become Benedictine nuns. The elder Beatrice founded a small convent on Monte Gemola in the Euganean Hills near Padua and died there young. Her niece, the younger Beatrice, was promised in marriage to a nobleman who was killed in battle before the wedding; she then became a nun and founded the convent of Sant'Antonio in Ferrara. The labels "B. Beat. I. Est." and "B. Beat. II. Est." call each of them "B." (Blessed), the title they still hold. This whole theca is a set of royal saints and saints of the House of Este, and may have been made for, or given by, a member of that house; that is a guess from the names, not a record.

**St. Benedict of Nursia (c. 480–547), Abbot.** Feast: 11 July (21 March, his death, in the monastic calendar). He left his studies in Rome to live as a hermit at Subiaco. Later he founded the monastery of Monte Cassino and wrote the Rule that this community, and Benedictines everywhere, still live by. It is a "little rule for beginners," as he called it, built on prayer, work, stability, and obedience, and it seasons strictness with mercy. St. Paul VI named him patron of Europe in 1964. Another relic is labelled "S. Benedicti Ab. C.", "of St. Benedict, Abbot and Confessor".

**St. Bernadette Soubirous (1844–1879), virgin.** Feast: 16 April (in France, 18 February). Bernadette was the sickly, barely schooled daughter of a poor miller's family in Lourdes when, in 1858, she said a lady appeared to her eighteen times in a grotto by the river, and on 25 March called herself "the Immaculate Conception", a phrase Bernadette did not understand. She refused every gift and offer of money, and in 1866 she joined the Sisters of Charity of Nevers, where she worked in the infirmary and the sacristy and was often ill herself; she died there of tuberculosis of the bone at 35, and was canonized in 1933. The label "S. M. Bernardae" means "of Saint Maria Bernarda", the name she took as a sister (Marie-Bernard). As with Fatima, the Church's approval of Lourdes (1862) is approval of a private revelation, which no one is bound to believe.

**St. Bernard of Clairvaux (1090–1153), Abbot and Doctor of the Church.** Feast: 20 August. Bernard came to Cîteaux at about 22 and brought some thirty relatives and friends with him. He was sent out within three years to found Clairvaux, which he led until his death. He was the great voice of the early Cistercians and of the whole Church of his day. He wrote on the love of God, preached on the Song of Songs, and ended a papal schism by winning Europe over to Innocent II. He also preached the Second Crusade, which failed and grieved him. He is called the "Mellifluous Doctor," the honey-sweet teacher, and many prayers to Our Lady come down to us in his words. The label's "Ab. D." means *Abbatis Doctoris*, "abbot and doctor." He is the patron of this abbey and school. The abbey has three more relics of him: one is labelled "S. Bernardi", one "S. Bern. A. D." ("Abbot and Doctor", which points clearly to St. Bernard of Clairvaux), and one sits on an oval holding fifteen labels.

**St. Blaise (died about 316), bishop and martyr.** Feast: 3 February. He is said to have been bishop of Sebaste in Armenia and to have been martyred under the Emperor Licinius, but almost nothing about him is historically certain. The best-known legend tells that he saved a boy who was choking on a fish bone. From that story comes the blessing of throats on his feast, given with two crossed candles.

**St. Bonaventure (c. 1217–1274), bishop and Doctor of the Church.** Feast: 15 July. Born at Bagnoregio, near Viterbo, Bonaventure became a Franciscan and a master of theology at Paris in the same years as St. Thomas Aquinas. In 1257 he was elected minister general of the Franciscans, held the quarrelling order together for seventeen years, and wrote its official life of St. Francis and short works on prayer such as *The Journey of the Mind into God*. He was made a cardinal in 1273 and died the next year during the Council of Lyon; he is called the "Seraphic Doctor".

**St. Camillus de Lellis (1550–1614), priest and founder.** Feast: 14 July (18 July in the United States). He was a hot-tempered soldier and a gambler who lost everything, even his shirt, before he was converted in 1575 while working on a building for the Capuchin friars. A sore on his leg that never healed took him to the hospital of San Giacomo in Rome, first as a patient and then as a worker, and he was shocked at how the sick were treated. He became a priest, with St. Philip Neri as his confessor, and founded the Ministers of the Sick (the Camillians), who wear a red cross and vow to serve the sick even at the risk of their lives, including in times of plague. He was canonized in 1746 and is a patron of the sick, of nurses and of hospitals.

**St. Catherine Labouré (1806–1876), virgin.** Feast: 28 November. A farmer's daughter from Burgundy who ran her father's house from the age of twelve, Catherine joined the Daughters of Charity, and in 1830, as a novice in their convent in the Rue du Bac in Paris, said that Our Lady appeared to her and asked her to have a medal made; it became known as the Miraculous Medal. She then spent some forty-five years caring for old men at a hospice outside Paris, minding the door and the poultry, and almost no one knew she was the sister who had seen the visions until shortly before her death. She was canonized in 1947. The apparitions are a private revelation, which no one is bound to believe; the medal itself is a sacramental, used with prayer.

**St. Catherine of Siena (1347–1380), virgin and Doctor of the Church.** Feast: 29 April. Catherine was the twenty-fourth child of a wool dyer in Siena; she refused to marry, joined the Dominican lay women called the Mantellate while still living at home, and nursed the sick and the dying. Though she learned to write only late, she dictated hundreds of letters and her book, *The Dialogue*, and she wrote and spoke boldly to Pope Gregory XI urging him to leave Avignon for Rome, which he did in 1377. She died in Rome at 33; she is a Doctor of the Church (1970) and a patron of Italy and of Europe.

**St. Charbel Makhlouf (1828–1898), Maronite monk and hermit.** Feast: 24 July. Born Youssef Makhlouf in a mountain village of Lebanon, he joined the Lebanese Maronite Order, was ordained priest, and lived at the monastery of St. Maron at Annaya. For his last twenty-three years he lived as a hermit near the monastery, praying, fasting and working the land. He died on Christmas Eve 1898, and after his death many reported that his body did not decay and that healings took place at his tomb. He was canonized in 1977.

**St. Clare of Assisi (1194–1253), founder of the Poor Clares.** Feast: 11 August. At eighteen she slipped out of her family's house at night to join St. Francis, who cut off her hair as a sign that she now belonged to God; she then led the community of poor sisters at San Damiano for over forty years. Tradition says that when soldiers attacked the convent she drove them back by holding up the Blessed Sacrament. She fought for years for the right of her sisters to own nothing at all, and the pope approved the rule she wrote two days before she died. She was canonized in 1255. "Ex capillis" means "from her hair".

**St. Claude de la Colombière (1641–1682), Jesuit priest.** Feast: 15 February. A French Jesuit, he was sent in 1675 to Paray-le-Monial, where he became the confessor of St. Margaret Mary Alacoque; he believed her account of her visions of the Sacred Heart and helped make the devotion known. He then went to London as preacher to the Duchess of York, was arrested during the panic of the "Popish Plot", and was banished from England. His health never recovered from prison, and he died at Paray at forty-one. The label "B. Cl. d. la Colom[bière]" calls him Blessed, so it was written between his beatification in 1929 and his canonization in 1992.

**Bl. Columba Marmion (1858–1923), abbot of Maredsous.** Feast: 3 October. Born Joseph Marmion in Dublin, he was a priest of that diocese before he entered the Benedictine abbey of Maredsous in Belgium in 1886, and he became its abbot in 1909. His talks to his monks became books, above all *Christ, the Life of the Soul*, which were read in monasteries and seminaries all over the world. During the First World War he took his young monks out of occupied Belgium to Ireland so they could carry on their training. The label "Ex Indumentis" means "from his clothing", a piece of cloth he wore, not a bone; and because it calls him "B." (Blessed), it was written after his beatification by John Paul II in 2000.

**St. Conrad of Parzham (1818–1894), Capuchin brother.** Feast: 21 April (Capuchin and German calendars). A Bavarian farmer's son, he joined the Capuchins as a lay brother and for more than forty years was the porter at their friary beside the shrine of Our Lady at Altötting. He spent his days answering the door to pilgrims, beggars and children, giving them food, a kind word and a little advice, and much of his nights in prayer. The label "B. Konradus à P[arzham]" calls him Blessed, so it was written between his beatification in 1930 and his canonization in 1934.

**St. Contardo d'Este (died 1249), pilgrim.** Feast: kept locally at Broni, near Pavia in northern Italy. A member of the noble house of Este, which ruled Ferrara, he set out as a poor pilgrim to the shrine of St. James at Compostela in Spain. He fell ill on the way at Broni and died there in a poor shelter, unknown, and the town has honoured him ever since as its patron. Almost all that is told of him comes from local tradition. The label "S. Contard. Est." means "of St. Contardo d'Este".

**St. Dominic (1170–1221).** Feast: 8 August. Domingo de Guzmán, as his label calls him (*de Guzmán* is his family name), was a Castilian canon. He was sent to southern France, where the Albigensian heresy had spread. He saw that preaching backed by money and pomp won no one, so he went on foot, begging, to preach and to argue in public. From that work came the Order of Preachers, approved in 1216. He sent his friars to the universities and the growing towns of Europe. The rosary is linked with his name by long tradition. That is a pious tradition, not a settled historical fact. The abbey has two more relics of him: one is labelled "S. Dominici C." ("of St. Dominic, Confessor"), and one sits on an oval holding fifteen labels.

**St. Dymphna (dates unknown), virgin and martyr.** Feast: 15 May (not in the General Calendar). Her story is a legend, first written down centuries after the events it tells: an Irish princess whose pagan father, mad with grief after his wife's death, wanted to marry her; she fled with a priest, Gerebernus, to Geel in what is now Belgium, where her father found them and killed her. What is history is Geel itself: since the Middle Ages people with mental illness have come to her shrine there, and the townspeople have taken them into their own homes, as many families still do. For that reason she is the patron of those who suffer from mental illness. This relic is on a modern plaque.

**St. Elizabeth Ann Seton (1774–1821).** Feast: 4 January. She was born into an Episcopalian family in New York. She married, had five children, and was widowed young, after her husband died of tuberculosis in Italy. There she first saw Catholic faith lived up close. She became a Catholic in 1805, against the wishes of most of her friends. In Emmitsburg, Maryland, she founded the Sisters of Charity of St. Joseph's and a school for girls, often called the beginning of Catholic parish schooling in the United States. In 1975 she became the first saint born in what is now the United States. A teacher and the mother of a family, she is a natural patron for a school. Another relic is labelled "Bd. E. A. Seton" (Blessed), so it was written between her beatification in 1963 and her canonization in 1975.

**St. Elizabeth of Hungary (1207–1231), princess and widow.** Feast: 17 November. She was a daughter of King Andrew II of Hungary and was married young to Louis, ruler of Thuringia in Germany. When Louis died on his way to the Crusade in 1227, she left court life, became a Franciscan tertiary, and spent her dowry on a hospital at Marburg, where she nursed the sick herself. She died there at twenty-four and was canonized only four years later, in 1235. The story of the bread in her cloak turning to roses is a legend. The label is in Italian, "S. Elisabetta d' Ung." (St. Elizabeth of Hungary), and "Ex Capil." means "from her hair".

**St. Elizabeth, queen.** The labels "S. Elisabeth R." and "s. Elisab. Reg." mean "of St. Elizabeth, Queen", and could be either of two saints, and the two labels may not even be the same one. St. Elizabeth of Hungary (1207–1231, feast 17 November) was a king's daughter who married the ruler of Thuringia, gave away food and money so freely that his family objected, and after his death built a hospital at Marburg and nursed the sick there until she died at 24. St. Elizabeth of Portugal (1271–1336, feast 4 July) was a crowned queen, the wife of King Denis, who more than once made peace between her husband and their rebellious son, once by riding out between the two armies; as a widow she lived beside the Poor Clares at Coimbra. Strictly the title "Queen" fits Elizabeth of Portugal, but labels did sometimes give it to Elizabeth of Hungary too.

**St. Eucharius (3rd century?), first bishop of Trier (likely).** Feast: 8 December at Trier (9 December in some calendars). The red ink on IMG_4910 has faded almost away, but its reliquary is the exact twin of the one labelled "S. Valerio" (IMG_4961): the same jewelled frame, the same kind of bone, the same hand writing in red. Read beside it, the faded strip is almost surely "S. Euchario". Tradition makes Eucharius the first bishop of Trier, with Valerius as his companion and successor; a medieval legend says St. Peter himself sent them. Both are buried at the Benedictine abbey of St. Matthias in Trier, and two more relics here point to that city: one labelled from the holy relics of "Abbatia S. Matth[iae]" (IMG_4964) and one of the Martyrs of Trier (IMG_4963). Together they look like one gift from Trier, perhaps from the monks of St. Matthias to the monks of St. Bernard.

**St. Eugene.** Feast: depends on which Eugene is meant. The label "S. Eugenii…" is cut off, and several saints bear the name, among them Pope St. Eugene I (died 657) and St. Eugene, bishop of Carthage, exiled by the Vandal kings (died 505). We do not know which of them this relic belongs to.

**St. Faustinus, martyr.** The label reads "…austini M." (of Faustinus, martyr), with the first letter hidden. Several early martyrs bore this name, among them Faustinus and Jovita of Brescia (feast 15 February) and Faustinus, martyred in Rome with his brother Simplicius and sister Beatrice (feast 29 July), and many relics from the Roman catacombs were also given this kind of name. Which Faustinus this relic belongs to is not known.

**St. Ferdinand III (1199–1252), king of Castile and León.** Feast: 30 May. He became king of Castile in 1217 and of León in 1230, joining the two kingdoms for good. He spent most of his reign at war with the Muslim rulers of southern Spain, taking Córdoba in 1236 and Seville in 1248. He was known for fair justice, for keeping the peace with the other Christian kings, and for building, among other things helping to begin the great cathedral of Burgos. He was canonized in 1671, and his body lies in Seville Cathedral; the label "S. Ferdin. R." means "of St. Ferdinand, King".

**St. Frances Xavier Cabrini (1850–1917), virgin.** Feast: 13 November (in the United States). The youngest of thirteen children in a Lombard farming family, Frances was turned down by two religious orders because of her poor health, and in 1880 founded her own, the Missionary Sisters of the Sacred Heart. She hoped to go to China, but Pope Leo XIII told her to go "not to the East, but to the West", and in 1889 she landed in New York to work among Italian immigrants. Though she was afraid of water, she crossed the ocean many times and founded schools, orphanages and hospitals across the Americas; she became a U.S. citizen in 1909, died in Chicago, and in 1946 was the first U.S. citizen to be canonized. The abbey has two of her relics, one labelled "Ex Indumentis", "from her clothing".

**St. Francis de Sales (1567–1622), bishop and Doctor of the Church.** Feast: 24 January. A nobleman of Savoy trained in law, he became a priest and went to win back the Calvinist region of the Chablais, writing leaflets and slipping them under doors when people would not come to hear him. As bishop of Geneva, living at Annecy, he wrote *Introduction to the Devout Life* to show that ordinary people in the world can be holy, and with St. Jane Frances de Chantal he founded the Visitation nuns. He was named a Doctor of the Church in 1877 and is patron of writers and journalists. The label "Ex Os. S. Franc. S. Ep." most likely stands for "from the bone of St. Francis de Sales, Bishop", though the abbreviation alone cannot rule out another Francis.

**St. Francis of Assisi (1181/82–1226), founder of the Friars Minor.** Feast: 4 October. The son of a rich cloth merchant, he gave his father back everything, even the clothes he stood in, in front of the bishop of Assisi, and set out to live the Gospel in complete poverty. Brothers gathered around him, and he sent them out to preach and to serve lepers. At Greccio in 1223 he set up a living Christmas crib, and on Mount La Verna in 1224 he received the stigmata, the wounds of Christ in his hands, feet and side. One of his three relics is labelled "Ex Cin. S. Franc. As.", which could mean "from the ashes" (*ex cineribus*, the remains of his body) or "from the cord" (*ex cingulo*, the knotted rope belt the friars wear); it is not certain which.

**St. Francis Xavier (1506–1552), priest and missionary.** Feast: 3 December. A nobleman from Navarre, he met St. Ignatius of Loyola as a student in Paris and was one of the first Jesuits. In 1541 he sailed for Portuguese India, and in the eleven years left to him he preached and baptized in Goa, along the south coast of India, in the islands of the East Indies and in Japan, often teaching the catechism by singing it with children in the street. He died of fever on the island of Shangchuan, off the coast of China, waiting for a ship to take him into the country. His body lies in Goa and his right arm in the Gesù in Rome; the label "Ex Os. S. Franc. Xav. C." means "from a bone of St. Francis Xavier, Confessor".

**St. Gabriel of Our Lady of Sorrows (1838–1862), religious.** Feast: 27 February. Francesco Possenti, son of a government official at Spoleto, was a lively young man who loved dancing, hunting and the theatre, and twice promised God during illness to enter religious life without keeping his word. In 1856, as an image of Our Lady was carried past him in procession, he felt called to go, and joined the Passionists, taking the name Gabriel of Our Lady of Sorrows. He died of tuberculosis at Isola del Gran Sasso at 23, before he could be ordained, and was canonized in 1920.

**St. Gemma Galgani (1878–1903).** The abbey has three of her relics: one in a jewelled oval (IMG_4905), one beside St. Gabriel of Our Lady of Sorrows in a silver almond-shaped frame (IMG_4973), and one labelled "Ex Corpore", from her body (IMG_4978). That pairing with Gabriel is apt: both belonged to the Passionist family, and she prayed to him. Feast: 11 April. She was a young woman of Lucca, in Italy, orphaned early and often ill. She wanted to be a Passionist nun, but her health kept her out. She lived quietly in a family that took her in, and she received extraordinary mystical graces, among them the stigmata. Her spiritual director, the Passionist Fr. Germanus, wrote her life. She died at 25 and was canonized in 1940. Her short life is a sign that holiness does not need a long life or a public one.

**St. Gerard Majella (1726–1755), Redemptorist brother.** Feast: 16 October. A tailor's son from southern Italy, he was turned away by the Capuchins as too frail, and was finally taken in by the Redemptorists as a lay brother. When a young woman falsely accused him of sin with her, he said nothing in his own defence and accepted his punishment until she took back the charge. Many stories of wonders were told about him, and he is asked to pray for mothers expecting a child because of one, about a handkerchief he left behind. He died of tuberculosis at twenty-nine and was canonized in 1904.

**St. Helena (c. 250–c. 330), empress.** Feast: 18 August. Helena was of humble birth, perhaps a stable-keeper's daughter, and the mother of Constantine, the first Christian emperor, who gave her the title Augusta; "Oss. S. Hel. Imp." means "from the bones of Saint Helena, Empress". History records that in old age she travelled to the Holy Land and that churches were built at Bethlehem and on the Mount of Olives in her time. The story that she found the True Cross in Jerusalem appears only some sixty years after her death, and is tradition, not proven history. Her empty porphyry coffin is in the Vatican Museums, and several churches, in Rome and elsewhere, claim her body.

**St. Henry II (973–1024), emperor.** Feast: 13 July. Duke of Bavaria, he became king of Germany in 1002 and was crowned emperor in Rome in 1014. He founded the diocese of Bamberg and its cathedral, where he and his wife, St. Cunegund, are buried; they had no children. He worked to reform the Church and its monasteries, and a story told of him says he once asked to become a monk at the abbey of Saint-Vanne in Verdun, and the abbot accepted him and then ordered him, under obedience, to go back and rule; for this he is the patron of Benedictine oblates. He was canonized in 1146; the label "S. Henrici" means "of St. Henry".

**St. Hermenegild (died 585), prince and martyr.** Feast: 13 April. He was the son of Leovigild, the Visigothic king of Spain, who held the Arian belief that the Son is not fully God. Through his Catholic wife Ingund and St. Leander of Seville, Hermenegild became Catholic, and he then rose in revolt against his father, was defeated and imprisoned. Pope St. Gregory the Great tells that at Easter he refused to receive communion from an Arian bishop and was put to death for it. Spanish writers of his own time saw a rebel rather than a martyr; but within a few years his brother, King Reccared, brought the Visigoths into the Catholic Church. The label "S. Hermeneg. R." means "of St. Hermenegild, King".

**St. Innocent.** Feast: unknown, since the saint is unknown. The label "Oss. S. Innocent." means "from the bones of Saint Innocent", in the singular, so it is one saint, not the Holy Innocents, the children killed by Herod (28 December). Several saints bore the name, among them Pope St. Innocent I (died 417), who led the Church of Rome when the Goths sacked the city in 410, and a number of early martyrs, some known only from a name on a Roman catacomb tomb. The label does not say which of them this is.

**St. Jacinta Marto (1910–1920) and St. Francisco Marto (1908–1919), seers of Fatima; and the Fatima tree.** Feast: 20 February, where it is kept (Portugal and some other places; it is not on the General Roman Calendar). With their cousin Lúcia dos Santos, these two shepherd children said that Our Lady appeared to them six times in 1917, on the 13th of each month from May to October, over a small holm oak (in Portuguese *azinheira*) at the Cova da Iria; Francisco died of the influenza epidemic in 1919 and Jacinta, after a painful illness, in a Lisbon hospital in 1920, and Pope Francis canonized them together at Fatima in 2017. The labels here read "HYACINTHAE" (of Jacinta, her name in Latin), "FRANCISCI" (of Francisco) and "ARBORIS" (of the tree); none of them says "Fatima", but the three names together make that reading almost certain. The tree relic would be a piece of that holm oak, which pilgrims stripped bare for keepsakes within months of the apparitions. The bishop of Leiria approved the apparitions in 1930 as worthy of belief: this is approval of a private revelation, which means the Church finds nothing in it against faith and allows devotion to it, but no Catholic is bound to believe it.

**St. Joachim (1st century B.C.), father of the Virgin Mary.** Feast: 26 July (with St. Anne). Like Anne, he is known only from the *Protoevangelium of James*, not from the Bible. There he is a rich and generous man who is turned away at the Temple because he has no children; ashamed, he goes into the desert to fast for forty days, and an angel tells him that Anne will bear a child. The label "Oss. S. Ioachin C." means "bones of St. Joachim, Confessor"; any bone relic of him rests on tradition only.

**St. John Berchmans (1599–1621), religious.** Feast: 13 August (the Jesuits keep 26 November). The son of a shoemaker in Diest, in what is now Belgium, John joined the Jesuits and was sent to study in Rome, where he died at 22, before he could be ordained. He did nothing remarkable outwardly: he was known for keeping the ordinary rules of community life cheerfully and exactly, and he died holding his crucifix, his rosary and his book of rules. He was canonized in 1888 and is a patron of altar servers.

**St. John Bosco (1815–1888), priest and founder of the Salesians.** Feast: 31 January. A poor farm boy from Piedmont, he learned juggling and conjuring tricks to draw other boys to hear the catechism. As a priest in Turin he gathered the city's street boys and young workers, gave them a place to play, pray, learn a trade and live, and founded the Salesians and, with St. Mary Mazzarello, the Daughters of Mary Help of Christians. His method was to win the young by kindness and reason rather than punishment. The label "B. Ioann. Bosco C." calls him Blessed and Confessor, so it was written between his beatification in 1929 and his canonization on Easter Sunday 1934.

**St. John Neumann (1811–1860), Redemptorist priest and bishop.** Feast: 5 January (in the United States). Born in Bohemia, he could not be ordained at home because there were too many priests, so he came to New York in 1836 and was ordained there for the German-speaking immigrants. He joined the Redemptorists and in 1852 became Bishop of Philadelphia, where he built churches and schools at great speed and organized the first diocesan Catholic school system in the country. He died suddenly, collapsing in the street, at forty-eight. In 1977 he became the first American bishop to be canonized.

**St. John the Baptist.** Feasts: his Birth, 24 June; his Passion (beheading), 29 August. He is the last of the prophets, the cousin of Jesus, who baptized him in the Jordan. He pointed to him with the words "Behold the Lamb of God," still said at every Mass. He was beheaded by Herod Antipas for condemning Herod's marriage to his brother's wife. His relics were already being venerated in the fourth century, and many churches claim parts of them. Whether any one of these old relics is truly his is a question for history, not for faith. The Church honors relics without binding anyone to believe in a given relic's authenticity. Another relic is labelled "Jo. Bapt. Prae[cursoris]", "of John the Baptist, the Forerunner", the title the Church gives him as the one who went before Christ to prepare his way.

**St. John Vianney (1786–1859), priest.** Feast: 4 August. John Vianney was a farmer's son who nearly failed his studies for the priesthood because he could not master Latin, and was sent in 1818 to Ars, a small, indifferent village near Lyon. He lived on very little, gave away almost everything he had, and spent up to sixteen hours a day hearing confessions as people came from all over France to see him. He was canonized in 1925 and is the patron of parish priests.

**St. Joseph (1st century), spouse of the Virgin Mary.** Feast: 19 March; St. Joseph the Worker, 1 May. The Gospels say little: he was a builder or carpenter of Nazareth, a just man, who took Mary as his wife and Jesus as his son, obeyed God's messages in dreams, and fled with them to Egypt to save the child from Herod. He is not mentioned after the finding of the boy Jesus in the Temple, and probably died before Jesus began to preach. No one knows where he was buried, so there are no known bones of St. Joseph; relics of him are said to be of his cloak or belt, and a ring in Perugia is shown as his wedding ring. The label "s. Josephi Sp." means "of St. Joseph, Spouse" (of the Blessed Virgin).

**St. Jude Thaddeus, apostle (first century).** Feast: 28 October (with St. Simon). Jude is one of the Twelve: Luke calls him "Judas son of James", Matthew and Mark call him Thaddaeus, and in John's Gospel "Judas (not Judas Iscariot)" asks Jesus at the Last Supper why he will show himself to the disciples and not to the world (John 14:22). The short Letter of Jude has traditionally been credited to him, though some scholars think it is by another Jude. Tradition says he preached in the East and was martyred with St. Simon; their relics are in St. Peter's Basilica. He is prayed to in hopeless cases, perhaps, it is often said, because his name was too like that of Judas Iscariot for people to turn to him except as a last resort.

**St. Justina, virgin and martyr.** Feast: unknown, since the saint is unknown (St. Justina of Padua is kept on 7 October). The label "S. Iustinae V.M." means "of Saint Justina, Virgin and Martyr", and several saints of the name fit it. The best known is St. Justina of Padua, said to have been martyred there around 304; the details of her life are legend, but her cult is old, and the Benedictine abbey of Santa Giustina in Padua led the great reform that became the Cassinese Congregation. The label does not say which Justina this is.

**St. Lawrence (died 258), deacon and martyr.** Feast: 10 August. Lawrence was one of the seven deacons of Rome, in charge of the Church's money and its care for the poor, and was killed a few days after Pope Sixtus II in the persecution of the Emperor Valerian. Tradition says that when ordered to hand over the Church's treasures he brought the poor and said, "These are the treasures of the Church", and that he was roasted on a gridiron and told his executioners to turn him over; many historians think he was in fact beheaded, like Sixtus. His tomb is at the basilica of San Lorenzo outside the Walls in Rome.

**St. Leonard of Port Maurice (1676–1751), priest.** Feast: 26 November. Leonard, a Franciscan friar from Porto Maurizio on the Ligurian coast, spent some forty years preaching parish missions all over Italy. He spread the Stations of the Cross, setting up hundreds of them, including the Stations in the Colosseum in Rome in 1750. He was beatified in 1796 and canonized in 1867.

**St. Lucy (died 304), virgin and martyr.** Feast: 13 December. She was put to death at Syracuse in Sicily in the persecution of Diocletian, and her name has been in the Roman Canon of the Mass since early times. Her story, written later, says she gave her dowry to the poor, and that the young man she had refused to marry denounced her as a Christian; when the guards came to drag her away, they could not move her. Because her name means "light", she became the patron of the blind and of eyes, and later pictures show her holding her eyes on a dish, a legend that is not in the early accounts. Her body has been in Venice since the 13th century.

**St. Luke, evangelist (first century).** Feast: 18 October. Luke wrote the Gospel that bears his name and the Acts of the Apostles, which together make up more than a quarter of the New Testament. He was a companion of St. Paul, who calls him "our dear doctor Luke" (Colossians 4:14), and the passages in Acts that suddenly say "we" are usually read as the places where he travelled with Paul. Tradition says he was a Gentile from Antioch; the story that he painted Mary is a legend. His body is venerated in the Benedictine basilica of Santa Giustina in Padua.

**St. Maria Goretti (1890–1902), virgin and martyr.** Feast: 6 July. Maria was the daughter of poor farm workers who moved to the marshes near Nettuno, south of Rome; after her father died she kept house and looked after the younger children while her mother worked in the fields. At eleven she refused the advances of Alessandro Serenelli, a young man of twenty who lived in the same house, and he stabbed her many times; before she died the next day she forgave him. Serenelli repented in prison, later asked her mother's forgiveness, and spent his last years as a lay helper in a Capuchin friary. Pius XII canonized her in 1950.

**St. Martin (probably Martin of Tours, c. 316–397), bishop.** Feast: 11 November. The label "S. Martini" ("of Saint Martin") does not say which Martin; Martin of Tours is the most likely, but Pope St. Martin I, who died in exile in 655, is another. Martin of Tours was a Roman soldier who, his early biographer says, cut his cloak in two to give half to a freezing beggar at Amiens, and that night dreamed of Christ wearing it. He left the army, founded one of the first monasteries in Gaul at Ligugé, and was made bishop of Tours in 371 against his wishes, living as a monk outside the city. He was one of the first saints to be honoured as a saint without being a martyr.

**St. Martin de Porres (1579–1639), Dominican brother.** Feast: 3 November. He was born in Lima, Peru, the son of a Spanish nobleman and a freed woman of African descent, and for years his father did not acknowledge him. He trained as a barber-surgeon and became a lay brother with the Dominicans, where he cared for the sick of the house and of the city, rich and poor, people of every race, and even stray animals. Stories of his being seen in two places at once and of his talking with mice belong to the tradition that grew up around him. He was canonized in 1962.

**The Martyrs of Trier.** Feast: kept locally at Trier. The label reads "[Ex] Oss. Martirum Trevir[ensium] … sub imper. Maximiano et […] martirio coronati sunt": "from the bones of the Martyrs of Trier, … who were crowned with martyrdom under the Emperor Maximian and […]"; the name after "et" is hidden. The legend says that around 286, under Maximian and his prefect Rictiovarus, Christian soldiers of the Theban Legion and many citizens of Trier were put to death there, and their remains are venerated in the church of St. Paulinus in Trier. That there were martyrs at Trier is likely, but the story as told, with its great numbers, was written down centuries later, and much of it is legend.

**St. Matilda (c. 895–968), queen.** Feast: 14 March. She was the wife of Henry the Fowler, king of Germany, and the mother of the Emperor Otto I. As a widow she gave so much to the poor and to churches that her own sons accused her of wasting the royal treasure, and for a time she was pushed out of the court. She founded several religious houses, among them the abbey of Quedlinburg, where she and her husband are buried. The label "S. Mehtildis R." gives an old German spelling of her name and calls her "R." (Queen), which tells her apart from the nuns St. Mechtild of Hackeborn and Mechtild of Magdeburg.

**St. Matthew the Apostle (first century), apostle and evangelist.** Feast: 21 September. He was a tax collector, a man his neighbours would have seen as a collaborator, when Jesus called him to follow, and Mark and Luke call him Levi. Tradition names him as the writer of the first Gospel. Where and how he died is not known; different traditions place his preaching and martyrdom in Ethiopia or Persia, and the cathedral of Salerno in Italy has claimed his body since the Middle Ages. The label "Ex Os. S. Mathei Ap." means "from the bone of St. Matthew, Apostle".

**St. Maurice (died c. 287?), martyr.** Feast: 22 September. The story, written down by Bishop Eucherius of Lyon about 150 years later, says that Maurice commanded the Theban Legion, Christian soldiers from Egypt, and that he and his men were killed at Agaunum in the Swiss Alps under the Emperor Maximian rather than take part in pagan sacrifice or kill fellow Christians. Historians think there were probably real martyrs at Agaunum, but the whole legion of over six thousand men is likely legend. The abbey of Saint-Maurice, founded over their tomb in 515, has been home to a religious community ever since.

**St. Nicholas of Bari (4th century), bishop of Myra.** Feast: 6 December. He was bishop of Myra, in what is now southern Turkey, and almost everything else told of him is legend. The best-known story is that he secretly threw three bags of gold through a poor man's window at night, so that the man's three daughters would have dowries and not be sold; from this grew his gift-giving and, in the end, Santa Claus. In 1087 sailors from Bari, in southern Italy, carried his bones off from Myra, and they have rested at Bari ever since, which is why the label calls him "S. Nicol. Bar." (of St. Nicholas of Bari). A clear liquid that gathers in the tomb, called the "manna" of St. Nicholas, is still collected there each year.

**St. Norbert (c. 1080–1134), bishop and founder.** Feast: 6 June. Norbert was a well-off canon at Xanten in the Rhineland, living comfortably on church income, until, his early life story says, he was thrown from his horse in a thunderstorm and turned to a life of penance and preaching. In 1120 he founded a community of canons at Prémontré in France, which became the Premonstratensian Order (Norbertines), and in 1126 he was made archbishop of Magdeburg, where he fought hard to reform the clergy. The label "Ex ossibus S. Norberti Ep. Conf." means "from the bones of Saint Norbert, Bishop and Confessor"; "confessor" was the old title for a saint who was not a martyr. His body was moved to the Strahov monastery in Prague in 1627 and is there still.

**St. Oswald (c. 604–642), king of Northumbria and martyr.** Feast: 5 August. As a young exile he lived among the monks of Iona in Scotland and was baptized there. In 634 he set up a wooden cross before the battle of Heavenfield, won the battle and his kingdom, and asked Iona for a bishop; St. Aidan came to Lindisfarne, and the king himself translated Aidan's sermons for his nobles, since the bishop did not yet know English. Bede tells that when Oswald gave the silver dish from his own table to the poor, Aidan took his right hand and said, "May this hand never perish." He was killed in battle against Penda of Mercia in 642 and his body was cut to pieces; his head is now in St. Cuthbert's tomb at Durham, and the label "S. Oswald R." means "of St. Oswald, King".

**St. Pacificus of San Severino (1653–1721), priest.** Feast: 24 September. Pacificus was a Franciscan friar in the Marche, in central Italy, who preached in the country villages until illness left him lame, nearly deaf and almost blind. He spent his last years in the friary at San Severino, praying and receiving the people who came to him. He was beatified in 1786 and canonized in 1839.

**St. Patrick (5th century), bishop.** Feast: 17 March. The label "Oss. S. Pat. Ep." means "from the bones of Saint Pat., Bishop"; "Pat." is most likely Patrick, though the abbreviation alone cannot rule out another bishop of a similar name. Patrick tells his own story in his *Confession*: born in Roman Britain, he was carried off by Irish raiders at sixteen and kept as a shepherd for six years, escaped, and later returned to Ireland as a bishop to preach to the people who had enslaved him. His exact dates are not known. The shamrock and the snakes are later legends.

**St. Paul of the Cross (1694–1775), priest and founder.** Feast: 19 October. Paolo Danei, a merchant's son from Ovada in Liguria, founded the Passionists, a congregation whose members promise to keep the memory of Christ's Passion and preach it; their first house was a retreat on Monte Argentario on the Tuscan coast. He spent his life preaching missions, often to the poor of the countryside, and his letters show that for many years he himself felt little comfort in prayer. He died in Rome and was canonized in 1867. St. Gabriel of Our Lady of Sorrows, above, was one of his Passionists.

**St. Paul the Apostle (died c. 64–67), apostle to the Gentiles.** Feast: 29 June (with St. Peter); his conversion is kept on 25 January. A Jew from Tarsus, a Pharisee and a tentmaker by trade, he hunted down the first Christians until the risen Jesus stopped him on the road to Damascus. He then spent some thirty years crossing the Roman world on foot and by ship, founding churches and writing the letters that make up much of the New Testament. By his own account he was flogged, stoned, shipwrecked and jailed many times. Tradition says he was beheaded in Rome, outside the city on the road to Ostia, where the Basilica of St. Paul Outside the Walls stands over his tomb.

**St. Peter the Apostle (died c. 64), first of the apostles.** Feast: 29 June (with St. Paul). Simon was a fisherman on the Sea of Galilee, living at Capernaum, when Jesus called him and gave him the name Peter, "rock". The Gospels do not hide his failures: he sank in the water, argued with Jesus about the cross, and denied him three times on the night of his arrest. He led the first church in Jerusalem and was put to death in Rome under Nero; the tradition is that he was crucified, and asked to be crucified head down. Excavations under St. Peter's Basilica in the 1940s found a grave the early Christians had honoured, and in 1968 Pope Paul VI said that bones found there were, in the Church's judgment, very probably Peter's.

**A St. Philip (IMG_4906, IMG_4907).** This is a large piece of bone with the name inked straight onto it, an older practice than paper labels. Only "S." and "Phili…" can be read. It could be St. Philip the Apostle (feast 3 May, with St. James), St. Philip Neri (26 May), St. Philip Benizi (23 August), or another Philip. The worn word before "Phili…" may have said which, but it cannot be read from the photos. A look in good, raking light, with a lamp held low and to the side, might bring it out.

**St. Philip Neri (1515–1595), priest.** Feast: 26 May. A Florentine who came to Rome as a young man and spent the rest of his life there, Philip met young people on the streets and in shops, heard confessions for hours each day, and gathered the priests and lay people who became the Congregation of the Oratory. He was known for his jokes and odd behaviour, such as going out with half his beard shaved, to keep people from thinking him holy. In 1544, praying in the catacomb of St. Sebastian, he felt what he described as a ball of fire enter his heart; for the rest of his life his heart beat so hard it could be seen, and after his death the doctors found it enlarged and two of his ribs pushed outward. The label "Ex praecordiis" means "from the area of the heart".

**St. Philip the Apostle (1st century), one of the Twelve.** Feast: 3 May (with St. James the Less). Philip came from Bethsaida, the town of Peter and Andrew, and brought his friend Nathanael to Jesus with the words "Come and see." In John's Gospel he is the one who works out that two hundred silver coins' worth of bread would not feed the crowd, and who asks at the Last Supper, "Lord, show us the Father." Tradition says he preached in Asia Minor and died at Hierapolis in Phrygia; relics said to be his are kept in the Church of the Holy Apostles in Rome. The label "Philippi Ap." (of Philip the Apostle) keeps him apart from Philip the deacon in Acts, with whom he was sometimes confused.

**St. Pier Giorgio Frassati (1901–1925), layman.** Feast: 4 July. The son of a wealthy Turin family (his father founded the newspaper *La Stampa*), he was a student, a keen mountaineer and a member of the St. Vincent de Paul Society, who quietly gave his money and time to the poor of the city. He died of polio at twenty-four, and his family was astonished at the crowds of poor people who came to his funeral. His words "Verso l'alto" ("To the heights") are remembered from his climbing. The label "B. PIER GEOR. F." calls him Blessed, so it was written between his beatification in 1990 and 2025; he was canonized on 7 September 2025.

**St. Pius X (1835–1914), pope.** Feast: 21 August. Giuseppe Sarto was the son of a village postman at Riese, near Venice, and worked as a parish priest, bishop of Mantua and patriarch of Venice before he was elected pope in 1903. He let children receive Communion from about the age of seven, urged everyone to receive it often, called for the return of Gregorian chant, and fought hard against the ideas he named "Modernism". The abbey has four of his relics, each in a different reliquary; one is labelled "B. Pius Pp. X" ("Blessed Pius, Pope, the Tenth"), which dates it between his beatification in 1951 and his canonization in 1954. His body lies in St. Peter's Basilica.

**St. Radegund (c. 520–587), queen and nun.** Feast: 13 August. A princess of Thuringia, she was carried off as a child by the Franks when they conquered her people, and was later made to marry King Clothar I. After her husband had her brother killed, she left him and was consecrated to God, and she founded the convent of the Holy Cross at Poitiers, where she lived as an ordinary nun. She obtained a relic of the True Cross from the emperor in Constantinople, and for its arrival her friend the poet Venantius Fortunatus wrote the hymn *Vexilla Regis*, still sung in Holy Week. The label "S. Radegundae R." means "of St. Radegund, Queen".

**St. Raymond Nonnatus (c. 1204–1240), Mercedarian friar.** Feast: 31 August (no longer on the General Roman Calendar since 1969, but kept by his order and in many places). His name, "not born", comes from the story that he was cut from his mother's womb after she died, which is why he is the patron of childbirth and midwives. He joined the Mercedarians, an order founded to buy back Christians held captive in North Africa, and the tradition is that when the ransom money ran out he gave himself as a hostage, and that his captors pierced his lips and padlocked them shut to stop him preaching. Most of his story comes from his order's later tradition, and little of it can be checked. The label "S. Raim. Non." means "of St. Raymond Nonnatus".

**St. Remigius (c. 437–c. 533), bishop of Reims.** Feast: 1 October (the traditional day; the Roman Martyrology now remembers him on 13 January, the day of his death). He was made bishop of Reims as a young layman and held the office for about seventy years. His great moment was the baptism of Clovis, king of the Franks, with thousands of his warriors, around the year 500, which made the Franks a Catholic people. Gregory of Tours tells that he said to the king at the font, "Bow your head, proud Sicambrian; worship what you have burned, and burn what you have worshipped." The label "S. Remigii Ep." means "of St. Remigius, bishop".

**St. Roch (14th century), pilgrim.** Feast: 16 August (not on the General Roman Calendar, but widely kept). The story, written down long after his death, says he was a young man from Montpellier in France who gave away his fortune, went on pilgrimage to Rome, and nursed people dying of plague on the way, curing many with the sign of the cross. When he caught the plague himself he went off into the woods to die, and a dog brought him bread each day until he recovered. Back home he was taken for a spy, thrown into prison, and died there without anyone knowing who he was. His dates and much of the story are uncertain; since 1485 his relics have been kept in Venice, and he is prayed to against plague and other epidemics.

**St. Sebastian (died c. 300?), martyr.** Feast: 20 January. Sebastian is certainly a real Roman martyr, buried in the catacomb on the Appian Way that bears his name and honoured there since the 4th century. The rest of his story comes from a later account and is legend: that he was an officer of the emperor's guard, was shot with arrows and left for dead, was nursed back to health by a widow named Irene, and then confronted the emperor and was beaten to death. Because of the arrows, people prayed to him against the plague.

**St. Secundus, Martyr.** This label, too, gives only the name and "M." Several early martyrs were named Secundus: St. Secundus of Asti, a soldier martyred under Hadrian around 119 (feast 30 March), and others in Africa and Italy. Like St. Agapitus beside him, he cannot be pinned down without the papers. The name means "second." In the Roman catacombs it was a common name, and so was Agapitus.

**St. Simon Stock (died 1265), Carmelite friar.** Feast: 16 May (Carmelite calendar). He was an Englishman and an early prior general of the Carmelites, at the time when the order, which began among hermits on Mount Carmel, was settling in Europe as friars. Tradition says that Our Lady appeared to him in 1251 and gave him the brown scapular with a promise for those who wear it; this story was first written down long after his death, and historians doubt it, though the Church approves the scapular as a sign of devotion to Mary. He died at Bordeaux.

**St. Teresa of Ávila (1515–1582), Carmelite nun and Doctor of the Church.** Feast: 15 October. In 1562 she founded a small, poor convent in Ávila to live the Carmelite rule more strictly, and she spent the rest of her life on the road founding more, with St. John of the Cross helping to bring the reform to the friars. She wrote of prayer from her own experience in her *Life* and *The Interior Castle*, plainly and often with humour. She died at Alba de Tormes on the night Spain changed to the new calendar, so the day after her death was dated 15 October. In 1970 she became the first woman named a Doctor of the Church. The label "S. Theres. a Iesu" uses her name in religion, Teresa of Jesus.

**St. Thomas Aquinas (about 1225–1274), Dominican priest and Doctor of the Church.** Feast: 28 January. As a boy he was placed with the Benedictines of Monte Cassino, and his noble family expected him to become its abbot; when he joined the new, begging Dominicans instead, his brothers seized him and kept him locked up for about a year. He went on to teach in Paris and Italy and wrote the *Summa Theologiae*, the most famous work of Catholic theology. In December 1273, after something he experienced at Mass, he stopped writing, saying that all he had written seemed like straw. He died a few months later at the Cistercian abbey of Fossanova and was canonized in 1323.

**St. Thomas of Cori (1655–1729), priest.** Feast: 11 January. Thomas was a shepherd boy at Cori, south of Rome, who became a Franciscan friar and spent most of his life at Bellegra, in the hills near Subiaco, where he founded houses of strict, quiet observance for the friars and preached in the villages around. He was beatified in 1786 and canonized in 1999. The labels in this reliquary call Angelo, Leonard, Pacificus and Thomas all "B." (Blessed): since Pacificus was canonized in 1839 and Angelo beatified in 1825, the labels were written between 1825 and 1839.

**The Uganda Martyrs: St. Charles Lwanga and St. Matthias Mulumba (both died 1886).** Feast: 3 June. In the 1880s King Mwanga II of Buganda turned on the Christians of his court, partly because the young pages who had become Christians refused his sexual demands. Charles Lwanga, head of the royal pages, had protected and taught the boys; he was burned to death at Namugongo on 3 June 1886 with many others. Matthias Mulumba, an older man and a local chief, was mutilated and left to die a few days earlier. Twenty-two Catholics were canonized together in 1964, and Anglican martyrs died alongside them. The label "Ex ossibus BB. Mart. Ugandensium" means "from the bones of the Blessed Martyrs of Uganda"; "BB." (Blessed) dates it between their beatification in 1920 and their canonization in 1964.

**St. Ursula (4th century?), virgin and martyr.** Feast: 21 October (no longer on the General Roman Calendar since 1969, but kept in many places). Almost nothing about her is history. An old stone in Cologne, from the 4th or 5th century, records that a man named Clematius rebuilt a church on the spot where holy virgins had been martyred; the legend grew from this into the story of a British princess killed by the Huns with 11,000 companions, a number that may come from misreading an abbreviation for eleven. In the 12th century a large Roman burial ground was dug up in Cologne and its bones were sent out across Europe as relics of Ursula and her companions, which is why so many churches have them. The two labels mean "of St. Ursula, virgin" and "from the bones of St. Ursula, virgin and martyr"; like all such relics, which person's bones they really are cannot be known.

**St. Valentine (dates unknown), martyr.** Feast: 14 February (no longer in the General Calendar). The label "s. Valen. M" most likely means "St. Valentine, Martyr". Very little is known about him: old lists name a priest of Rome and a bishop of Terni called Valentine, both martyred and buried on the Via Flaminia, and they may be one man. The stories about him marrying couples or writing to his jailer's daughter are legends, and the link with romantic love comes from the late Middle Ages, not from his life. Because so little is certain, his feast was dropped from the General Roman Calendar in 1969, though he remains in the Roman Martyrology.

**St. Valerius of Trier (died early 4th century?), bishop.** Feast: 29 January (at Trier). Tradition makes Valerius the companion and successor of St. Eucharius, the first bishops of Trier, in what is now western Germany; a medieval legend added that St. Peter himself sent them. Almost nothing about him is known for certain, but his tomb, with that of Eucharius, is still venerated at the Benedictine abbey of St. Matthias in Trier. The label "S. Valerio" is written in red ink.

**St. Vincent Ferrer (1350–1419), Dominican priest.** Feast: 5 April. Born in Valencia, he spent his last twenty years walking through Spain, France, Switzerland and Italy preaching repentance before the coming judgment, followed by crowds that included penitents scourging themselves. During the Great Western Schism he backed the Avignon pope, Benedict XIII, but in 1416 he withdrew his support, which helped bring the schism to an end. His campaigns also put heavy pressure on the Jews of Spain, and many converted under it; that is part of his story too. He died at Vannes in Brittany and was canonized in 1455.
