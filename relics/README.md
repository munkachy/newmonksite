# Relics

Photographs of the abbey's relics, taken 1 November 2026 and brought in from the "Relics" folder in Google Drive.

- `originals/`: the photos exactly as taken. Only the GPS location was removed; the picture itself is untouched.
- `labeled/`: an upright copy of each photo with the saint's name, the words on the label, and how sure the reading is,
  written across the bottom. The same name is also saved in the file's details (Title, Description, and Tags, which
  show in Windows "Properties" and in most photo apps).

Readings come from the photos alone. Where a label is cut off, faded or hidden, the list says so. Please check the
uncertain ones against the relics themselves, and against any authentication papers (*authentica*) the abbey keeps.

## The saints

**St. Bernard of Clairvaux (1090–1153), Abbot and Doctor of the Church.** Feast: 20 August. Bernard came to Cîteaux
at about 22 and brought some thirty relatives and friends with him. He was sent out within three years to found
Clairvaux, which he led until his death. He was the great voice of the early Cistercians and of the whole Church of
his day. He wrote on the love of God, preached on the Song of Songs, and ended a papal schism by winning Europe over to Innocent II. He also
preached the Second Crusade, which failed and grieved him. He is called the "Mellifluous Doctor," the
honey-sweet teacher, and many prayers to Our Lady come down to us in his words. The label's "Ab. D." means *Abbatis
Doctoris*, "abbot and doctor." He is the patron of this abbey and school.

**St. Elizabeth Ann Seton (1774–1821).** Feast: 4 January. She was born into an Episcopalian family in New York. She
married, had five children, and was widowed young, after her husband died of tuberculosis in Italy. There she first
saw Catholic faith lived up close. She became a Catholic in 1805, against the wishes of most of her friends. In
Emmitsburg, Maryland, she founded the Sisters of Charity of St. Joseph's and a school for girls, often called the beginning of
Catholic parish schooling in the United States. In 1975 she became the first saint born in what is now the United
States. A teacher and the mother of a family, she is a natural patron for a school.

**St. Agapitus, Martyr.** The label gives only the name and "M." for martyr, and several early martyrs bore this name.
The two best known are St. Agapitus of Palestrina (Praeneste), a boy martyred near Rome in the third century (feast
18 August), and St. Agapitus the deacon, killed with Pope St. Sixtus II in 258 (6 August). Small paper labels written
by hand, set on silk in a wooden case, are typical of relics given out from Rome in the 1700s and 1800s. Many of
those came from the Roman catacombs. So this may be either of these men or another martyr of the same name. Only
the relic's papers could settle it.

**St. Secundus, Martyr.** This label, too, gives only the name and "M." Several early martyrs were named Secundus:
St. Secundus of Asti, a soldier martyred under Hadrian around 119 (feast 30 March), and others in Africa and Italy.
Like St. Agapitus beside him, he cannot be pinned down without the papers. The name means "second." In the Roman
catacombs it was a common name, and so was Agapitus.

**St. Dominic (1170–1221).** Feast: 8 August. Domingo de Guzmán, as his label calls him (*de Guzmán* is his family
name), was a Castilian canon. He was sent to southern France, where the Albigensian heresy had spread. He saw that
preaching backed by money and pomp won no one, so he went on foot, begging, to preach and to argue in public. From
that work came the Order of Preachers, approved in 1216. He sent his friars to the universities and the growing towns
of Europe. The rosary is linked with his name by long tradition. That is a pious tradition, not a settled historical
fact.

**The tomb of the Blessed Virgin Mary ("Ex Sepulchro B. V. M.").** This is not a relic of Mary's body, and that is
not an accident. That Mary was taken up body and soul into heavenly glory is **dogma** (Pius XII, *Munificentissimus
Deus*, 1950). The Church teaches that no bodily relics of her exist. Whether she died before her Assumption was
deliberately left undefined. That she did, the "Dormition," is the older and more common view, but it remains
**theological opinion**. What pilgrims could bring home was a fragment from the tomb venerated in Jerusalem, in the
Kidron Valley beside Gethsemane, or a cloth touched to it. "Ex sepulchro" means "from the tomb." It is a relic of
the place, a witness that her body was laid there and is no longer there.

**St. John the Baptist.** Feasts: his Birth, 24 June; his Passion (beheading), 29 August. He is the last of the
prophets, the cousin of Jesus, who baptized him in the Jordan. He pointed to him with the words "Behold the Lamb of
God," still said at every Mass. He was beheaded by Herod Antipas for condemning Herod's marriage to his brother's
wife. His relics were already being venerated in the fourth century, and many churches claim parts of them. Whether
any one of these old relics is truly his is a question for history, not for faith. The Church honors relics without
binding anyone to believe in a given relic's authenticity.

**St. Gemma Galgani (1878–1903).** Feast: 11 April. She was a young woman of Lucca, in Italy,
orphaned early and often ill. She wanted to be a Passionist nun, but her health kept her out. She lived quietly in a
family that took her in, and she received extraordinary mystical graces, among them the stigmata. Her spiritual
director, the Passionist Fr. Germanus, wrote her life. She died at 25 and was canonized in 1940. Her short life is a
sign that holiness does not need a long life or a public one.

**A St. Philip (IMG_4906, IMG_4907).** This is a large piece of bone with the name inked straight onto it, an older
practice than paper labels. Only "S." and "Phili…" can be read. It could be St. Philip the Apostle (feast 3 May,
with St. James), St. Philip Neri (26 May), St. Philip Benizi (23 August), or another Philip. The worn word before
"Phili…" may have said which, but it cannot be read from the photos. A look in good, raking light, with a lamp held
low and to the side, might bring it out.

**Probably St. Eucharius (IMG_4910).** The red ink has faded to almost nothing, but the letters read most easily as
"S. Euchar…" If that is right, this is most likely St. Eucharius, traditionally the first bishop of Trier in Germany,
in the third century (feast 8 or 9 December). His relics have rested at the abbey of St. Matthias in Trier, a
Benedictine house, since the Middle Ages. This reading should be checked against the relic itself before it is used
anywhere.

**St. Benedict of Nursia (c. 480–547), Abbot.** Feast: 11 July (21 March, his death, in the monastic calendar). He
left his studies in Rome to live as a hermit at Subiaco. Later he founded the monastery of Monte Cassino and wrote
the Rule that this community, and Benedictines everywhere, still live by. It is a "little rule for beginners," as he
called it, built on prayer, work, stability, and obedience, and it seasons strictness with mercy. St. Paul VI named
him patron of Europe in 1964.
